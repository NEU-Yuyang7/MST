\title{BMSBorůvka: A Multi-Level Bucket Framework for Minimum Spanning Tree}

\begin{abstract}
Classical minimum spanning tree (MST) algorithms such as
Kruskal\textquotesingle s and Prim\textquotesingle s run in
\(O(m \log n)\) time, a cost that comes from comparison-based ordering:
sorting all edges in Kruskal\textquotesingle s algorithm and
priority-queue operations in Prim\textquotesingle s.
Borůvka\textquotesingle s algorithm needs no ordering, but performs
\(O(\log n)\) rounds over the remaining edges and so has the same
\(O(m \log n)\) bound. In this paper, we propose BMSBorůvka, a
bucket-based Borůvka-style algorithm that avoids full sorting through
phased edge activation and multi-level graph compression. Our approach
runs in \(O(m \log n)\) time in the worst case, and in
\(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) time under a bounded carry-over condition between
phases, which held on every graph in our experiments. In our
experiments, BMSBorůvka is consistently faster than
Borůvka\textquotesingle s algorithm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A minimum spanning tree (MST) or minimum weight spanning tree is a
subset of the edges of a connected, edge-weighted undirected graph that
connects all the vertices together, without any cycles and with the
minimum possible total edge weight. Classical algorithms such as Prim's
and Kruskal's algorithms run in \(O(m \log n)\) time, where \(n\) is the
number of vertices and \(m\) the number of edges. Their cost is dominated
by comparison-based ordering---either repeated priority queue operations
in Prim's algorithm or explicitly sorting all edges by weight in
Kruskal's algorithm. Borůvka's algorithm {[}Bor26{]} needs no ordering,
but performs \(O(\log n)\) rounds, each scanning every remaining edge,
and therefore has the same bound.

Asymptotically faster algorithms are known. Fredman and Tarjan's
Fibonacci-heap algorithm runs in \(O(m\,\beta(m,n))\) time {[}FT87{]},
Chazelle's in \(O(m\,\alpha(m,n))\) {[}Cha00{]}, and the randomized
algorithm of Karger, Klein and Tarjan in expected linear time
{[}KKT95{]}. These algorithms are rarely used in practice because of
their complexity and constant factors. This paper works within the
Borůvka framework: we reduce the cost of its rounds while keeping its
simplicity, and compare against Borůvka's algorithm and the practical
algorithms above.

This reliance reflects a broader sorting bottleneck that also appears in
shortest path algorithms. It has long been known that when values can be
classified in constant time, sorting can be performed in linear time,
avoiding comparison-based ordering. Recent work {[}DMMS+25{]} on
breaking the sorting barrier for directed single-source shortest paths
exploits this idea by replacing comparison-heavy priority queue
operations with classification-based techniques using multi-level
buckets and graph sparsification. Motivated by this approach, we extend
similar ideas to the Borůvka framework. The resulting algorithm keeps
Borůvka's \(O(m \log n)\) worst-case bound, runs in
\(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) time under a bounded carry-over condition, and is
consistently faster than Borůvka's algorithm in our experiments.

\section{Related Work}

The minimum spanning tree (MST) problem is one of the most fundamental
problems in graph algorithms, with a long line of research focusing on
improving its time complexity. Classical algorithms such as Kruskal
{[}Kru56{]} and Prim~{[}Pri57{]} algorithms achieve \(O(m \log n)\)
time by relying on comparison-based sorting or priority queue
operations. Borůvka's algorithm {[}Bor26{]}, the oldest of the three,
instead proceeds in rounds in which every component selects its
cheapest outgoing edge; each round at least halves the number of
components, so \(O(\log n)\) rounds of \(O(m)\) work give the same
\(O(m \log n)\) bound. Borůvka's round structure is the starting point
of this work.

The first improvement on \(O(m \log n)\) for general graphs came from
Fredman and Tarjan {[}FT87{]}. Using Fibonacci heaps, Prim's algorithm
runs in \(O(m + n \log n)\), and by growing many trees with a bounded
heap size in each pass, their MST algorithm runs in
\(O(m\,\beta(m,n))\) time, where
\(\beta(m,n) = \min\{\, i : \log^{(i)} n \le m/n \,\}\). The function
\(\beta\) grows extremely slowly: for every graph in our experiments
\(\beta(m,n) \le 2\). Fredman and Tarjan's algorithm is therefore
already asymptotically faster than the bounds we prove for BMSBorůvka;
our contribution lies within Borůvka-style algorithms, not in the
general comparison-based bound.

A major line of work has sought to reduce or bypass the sorting
bottleneck inherent in these approaches. Chazelle {[}Cha00{]} gave a
deterministic MST algorithm running in \(O(m\ \alpha(m,\ n))\) time,
where α is the inverse Ackermann function. His algorithm avoids explicit
global sorting by using soft heaps, effectively allowing controlled key
corruption to achieve near-linear performance.

Randomization has also played a crucial role in overcoming the sorting
barrier. Karger, Klein, and Tarjan {[}KKT95{]} presented a randomized
linear-time MST algorithm with expected running time \(O(m)\), combining
random sampling, filtering, and contraction techniques. This result
demonstrates that, in the randomized setting, the sorting barrier can be
circumvented entirely.

Pettie and Ramachandran {[}PR02{]} further advanced the theoretical
understanding of MST by designing an optimal comparison-based algorithm.
Their algorithm achieves a running time of \(O(m\ \alpha(m,\ n))\) plus
a term matching the decision-tree complexity of MST, thereby showing
that MST can be solved optimally in the comparison model without
requiring explicit sorting.

On the practical side, the fastest MST codes are engineered variants of
the classical algorithms rather than the asymptotically best ones.
Filter-Kruskal {[}OSS09{]} partitions the edges around a pivot weight,
recurses on the light half first, and discards heavy edges whose
endpoints are already connected before they are ever sorted; on random
weights this removes most of the sorting work. We use Filter-Kruskal,
Kruskal, Prim with a binary heap, and Borůvka as the baselines in
Section~5.

Despite these advances, the near-linear and optimal algorithms rely on
sophisticated data structures or randomization and are rarely used in
practice, while the practical algorithms remain at \(O(m \log n)\) in
the worst case. Recent work on single-source shortest paths (SSSP) has
explored breaking the sorting barrier through hierarchical bucketing and
sparsification techniques {[}DMMS+25{]}. Inspired by these developments,
our work investigates whether similar ideas can improve Borůvka-style
MST construction.

In particular, we propose a bucket-based multi-level sparsification
framework that activates edges in phases by weight and progressively
contracts the graph while preserving candidate MST edges. This approach
avoids global edge sorting and keeps the simple round structure of
Borůvka's algorithm. It runs in \(O(m \log n)\) time in the worst case
and in \(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) time under a bounded carry-over condition
(Section~4.3). Our goal is a simple and practically efficient
improvement to Borůvka's algorithm, not a new general bound for MST.

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Graphs and the Minimum Spanning Tree Problem}

An undirected weighted graph is a triple \(G = (V,E,w)\) where V is a
finite set of vertices,
\(E\  \subseteq \text{\{\{}u,v\text{\}}\  \mid u,v\  \in V,\ u\  \neq v\text{\}}\ \)is
a set of edges, and \(w:E \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}\) assigns an integer
weight to each edge. We write \(n\  = \ |V|\) and \(m\  = \ |E|\).

A path between two vertices u and v is a sequence of distinct vertices u
= v₀, v₁, \ldots, vₖ = v such that
\(\left\{ v_{i},\ v_{i + 1} \right\} \in \ E\) for each i. A graph is
connected if a path exists between every pair of vertices; otherwise it
is disconnected.

A spanning tree connects all vertices of a graph with no cycles; when
edges carry weights, the spanning tree that minimises the sum of edge
weights is called the minimum spanning tree (MST) {[}Hil24{]}. A
spanning tree of a connected graph G is a subgraph \(T\  \subseteq E\)
that (i) connects all n vertices, (ii) contains no cycles, and (iii) has
exactly \(n - 1\) edges. A minimum spanning tree (MST) is a spanning
tree whose total edge weight \(\sum_{e \in T}^{}{w(e)}\) is minimised
over all spanning trees. If G is disconnected with \(c\) connected
components, the analogous structure is a minimum spanning forest (MSF),
which has \(n - c\) edges.

Two classical properties are central to all MST algorithms:

\textbf{Cut Property.} Let \(S\  \subset V\) be any non-empty proper
subset of vertices. The cut defined by S consists of all edges with one
endpoint in S and the other in \(V\  \smallsetminus S\). If e is the
unique minimum-weight edge of this cut, then e belongs to every MST.
More generally, if a partial MST contains no edge of the cut, any
minimum-weight edge across the cut can be added to it without creating
a suboptimal solution.

\textbf{Cycle Property.} If e is the unique maximum-weight edge on some
cycle in G, then e does not belong to any MST.

These two properties together characterise exactly the edges that belong
to the MST. Both are stated for distinct weights; with ties broken by a
fixed total order on edges (Section~3.2), all weights can be treated as
distinct.

\subsection{Borůvka\textquotesingle s Algorithm}

Borůvka\textquotesingle s algorithm {[}Bor26{]} is the foundation on
which BMSBoruvka is built. The key idea is to grow many components of
the MST simultaneously rather than one edge at a time.

Starting with n isolated components (one per vertex), the algorithm
repeats the following Borůvka round until only one component remains:

\textbf{Find cheapest outgoing edge.} For each current component,
identify its minimum-weight edge that crosses to a different component
(its cheapest outgoing edge, or COE). Break ties deterministically,
using a fixed total order on edges: by weight, then by endpoints, then
by original edge index.

\textbf{Merge.} Add every selected COE to the MST and merge the two
components it connects. Because multiple components may select the same
edge, a Disjoint Set Union structure (Section 3.4) tracks which
components have already merged.

After one round, every component that had an outgoing edge has merged
with at least one neighbour, so the number of components at least halves
per round. Starting from n components and halving each round, the
algorithm terminates in at most ⌈log₂ n⌉ rounds. Each round scans all m
edges, giving a total time of \(O(m \log n)\) for a connected graph.

\subsection{Graph Compression (Super-nodes and Super-edges)}

After several Borůvka rounds the number of components has shrunk
significantly --- for example, from 1,000,000 vertices down to a few
hundred "super-nodes". It is wasteful to keep scanning all m original
edges when most of them now connect vertices within the same super-node
and can never be in the MST.

Graph compression solves this by replacing the current graph with a
smaller compressed graph whose vertices are the surviving super-nodes.
More precisely, if two super-nodes A and B are connected by several
original edges, we keep only the minimum-weight one (by the cycle
property, all others are dominated). Edges within a single super-node
become self-loops and are discarded.

After compression the new graph has C vertices (one per remaining
super-node) and at most one edge per pair of super-nodes; at most
\(C - 1\) of these edges can belong to the MST. Subsequent Borůvka
rounds therefore operate on a much smaller edge set; Section~4.3 bounds
how many edges are carried from one super-step into the next.

The algorithm maintains a mapping origToComp{[}v{]} that records, for
each original vertex v, the ID of the super-node it currently belongs
to. This allows new edges to be pushed into the compressed graph without
re-reading the original edge array.

\subsection{Disjoint Set Union (DSU)}

A Disjoint Set Union (DSU), also called Union-Find, is a data structure
that efficiently tracks which elements belong to the same set
(component). It supports two operations:

\textbf{find(x)} --- returns the representative (root) of the set
containing x. Two elements are in the same component if and only if
their representatives are equal.

\textbf{unite(a, b)} --- merges the sets containing a and b, returning
the new representative.

This implementation uses two standard optimisations. Union by rank
{[}Tar75{]} always attaches the shorter tree under the taller one,
keeping trees shallow. Path halving {[}TvL84{]} during find() rewires
every other node on the path directly to its grandparent, compressing
future traversals. Together these give an amortised cost of
\(O(\alpha(n))\) per operation, where α is the inverse Ackermann
function --- a value no larger than 4 for any input that fits in
computer memory. For all practical purposes this is \(O(1)\).

Crucially, find() is not a comparison in the graph-theory sense --- it
performs pointer traversals through the parent{[}{]} array. This
distinction matters for the cost model (Section 3.6).

\subsection{Selection vs. Sorting: nth\_element}

A classical result in algorithm theory is that selecting the k-th
smallest element of an array of n items requires only \(O(n)\)
comparisons in the worst case, whereas sorting the entire array requires
\(\Omega\left( n\log n \right)\) comparisons. This gap of a factor log n
is exactly what BMSBoruvka exploits.

The C++ standard library function std::nth\_element(first, nth, last,
cmp) rearranges the range {[}first, last) so that:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  The element at position nth is the element that would be there in
  sorted order.
\item
  Every element before nth is no greater than the element at nth.
\item
  Every element after nth is no less than the element at nth.
\item
  Elements before nth and elements after nth are not sorted relative to
  each other.
\end{itemize}

The C++ standard requires this operation to run in linear time on
average; common implementations use Introselect, a Quickselect variant
that falls back to a slower method when recursion becomes too deep, and
are not guaranteed linear in the worst case. A worst-case \(O(n)\)
bound is achievable with the median-of-medians algorithm, and for
integer weights a radix-based partition gives the same bound; our
implementation offers the latter as an option
(\texttt{--phase-a=radix}). The analysis in Section~4 assumes a
linear-time selection step.

In BMSBoruvka, nth\_element is called once per super-step on the
unactivated suffix of the edge array. It moves the lightest k edges to
the front (unordered) so they can be processed in that super-step, while
deferring heavier edges to later super-steps. This preserves the
weight-prefix property needed for correctness (Section 4.2) without
paying the \(O(m \log m)\) cost of a full global sort.

\subsection{Cost Model}

We analyse BMSBoruvka under the unit-cost word RAM model. In this model
every elementary operation on a machine word --- integer arithmetic,
array indexing, pointer dereference, hash-table lookup --- costs exactly
1 unit of time, regardless of its type. All integers, edge weights, and
array indices fit in a single word.

Under this model:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  An edge-weight comparison costs 1 unit.
\item
  A DSU find() call costs \(O(\alpha(n))\) units (amortised over any
  sequence of operations), which we treat as \(O(1)\) throughout the
  analysis since \(\alpha(n) \leq 4\) in practice.
\item
  A hash\_map insertion or lookup costs \(O(1)\) units amortised.
\item
  Allocating or writing a block of k memory words costs \(O(k)\) units.
\end{itemize}

This model is more general than the pure comparison model used in
classical MST lower bounds (which counts only edge-weight comparisons).
The distinction matters here because Phase B performs DSU operations in
addition to comparisons, and Phase C additionally incurs hash-table
costs. The RAM model captures all of
these.

The RAM model does not capture cache effects. In practice, find()
accesses the parent{[}{]} array in a graph-dependent, near-random order;
for large n (e.g., n = 10\^{}6, parent{[}{]} occupying 4 MB) this causes
L2/L3 cache pressure that is invisible to the RAM model. Measured
running times therefore need not follow the RAM-model bounds exactly;
Section~5 discusses how the normalised throughput \(\widehat{t}\)
behaves in practice.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The Algorithm}

The BMSBoruvka algorithm operates on an undirected weighted graph
\(G = (V,\ E)\) with \(n\  = \ |V|\) vertices and \(m\  = \ |E|\) edges.
Two integer parameters govern the structure of the computation:

\begin{quote}
\(t\  = \ \left\lceil \log_{2}^{\frac{2}{3}}(n) \right\rceil\  \geq \ 1\)
(Borůvka rounds per super-step)

\(P = \left\lceil \log_{2}(n)\text{/}t \right\rceil = \Theta\left( \log^{1\text{/}3}n \right)\)
(number of super-steps)
\end{quote}

The algorithm proceeds in P super-steps. Each super-step consists of
three phases:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Phase A (Activation).} A call to nth\_element partitions the
  as-yet unactivated suffix of edges{[}{]} so that the k = ⌊m/P⌋
  lightest edges move to the front (unordered among themselves) and
  every remaining edge is no lighter than any activated edge. These are
  pushed into curEdges via the origToComp{[}{]} mapping and the DSU. Because the
  activated prefix is always a weight-prefix of E, the cut property is
  preserved.
\item
  \textbf{Phase B (Borůvka Rounds).} Execute t standard Borůvka rounds
  on curEdges. In each round every component selects its minimum-weight
  outgoing edge, all selected edges are added to the MST, and edges
  whose endpoints have been merged are filtered out.
\item
  \textbf{Phase C (Compression).} compress() renumbers the surviving
  super-nodes 0, \ldots, Cᵢ₊₁−1, rebuilds curEdges with the new IDs
  (discarding self-loops), and updates origToComp{[}0..n). The DSU is
  then reinitialised in the compressed space.
\end{enumerate}

The outer loop terminates when all \(n-1\) MST edges have been
collected or uf.comps drops to 1. If components with outgoing edges
remain after super-step \(P-1\), which activates all remaining edges,
further super-steps run on the already-activated edges until curEdges is
empty; Lemma 2 bounds their number.

\subsection{Correctness Analysis}

\begin{theorem}[MST / MSF Correctness]
BMSBoruvka returns a
minimum spanning tree of G if G is connected, and a minimum spanning
forest otherwise.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We verify the three invariants that together guarantee
correctness.

\textbf{Cut property preservation.} nth\_element guarantees that
edges{[}curActivated..activateEnd) contains the globally lightest k
unactivated edges: every edge in the window has weight ≤ every edge
outside it. Because curActivated advances monotonically across
super-steps, the cumulative activated set is always a weight-prefix of
E. The cut property requires only that the minimum-weight crossing edge
of each cut be present in curEdges at the moment that cut is resolved;
since the activated set is a weight-prefix, this condition is always
satisfied.

\textbf{Phase B safety.} Each Borůvka round selects, for every active
component, its cheapest outgoing edge in curEdges under a total order on
edges (weight, then endpoints, then original index), which is fixed for
the duration of the round. Because the activated set is a weight-prefix
of \(E\), this edge is also a cheapest edge of the component's cut in
\(G\), so by the cut property it is safe. Selected edges are added
through the DSU, whose unite() rejects any edge whose endpoints are
already connected, so no cycle is formed and no edge is added twice.

\textbf{Compression correctness.} Every edge in the compressed graph
corresponds to a unique original edge via orig\_eidx. Self-loops (edges
whose endpoints lie in the same component) are discarded, which is sound
because a self-loop cannot belong to any spanning tree. The
origToComp{[}{]} mapping is updated consistently so that future Phase A
activations route edges to the correct super-nodes.

For disconnected graphs the outer loop terminates once no component has
an outgoing edge in curEdges; the result is a minimum spanning forest
with \(n - c\) edges, where c is the number of connected components.
Correctness was independently verified by 3,000 random stress tests
against Kruskal's algorithm.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Time Complexity Analysis}

Write \(A_i^{(r)}\) for the number of components that have at least one
outgoing edge in curEdges at the start of round \(r\) of super-step
\(i\), and \(m_i'\) for the number of edges carried from super-step
\(i\) into super-step \(i+1\) (the size of curEdges after compression).
We first note that the shrinkage of all components,
\(C_{i+1} \le C_i/2^{t}\), does \emph{not} hold in general: a super-step
activates only \(k = \lfloor m/P \rfloor\) new edges and every merge
consumes one edge, so \(C_1 \ge n - k\). For \(m = 2n\) and
\(n = 10^6\) this gives \(C_1 \ge 0.33\,n\), against \(n/2^{t} = n/256\).
What does hold is the shrinkage of the components that still have work
to do.

\begin{lemma}[Active-component shrinkage]
Within Phase B of any super-step, \(A_i^{(r+1)} \le A_i^{(r)}/2\).
Consequently, after the \(t\) rounds of super-step \(i\), the carried
edges have their endpoints in at most \(C_i/2^{t} \le n/2^{t}\)
components.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A component \(X\) counted in \(A_i^{(r)}\) selects an edge to some
component \(Y\); that edge is also an outgoing edge of \(Y\), so \(Y\)
is counted as well, and after the round \(X\) and \(Y\) lie in the same
component. Hence every component formed in round \(r\) that contains a
counted component contains at least two of them, and
\(A_i^{(r+1)} \le A_i^{(r)}/2\). Components without an outgoing edge in
curEdges cannot gain one during Phase B, since Phase B only removes
edges. After \(t\) rounds, \(A_i^{(t)} \le A_i^{(0)}/2^{t} \le
C_i/2^{t}\), and every carried edge joins two of these components.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Number of super-steps]
BMSBoruvka performs at most \(2P = O(\log^{1/3} n)\) super-steps.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Super-steps \(0,\dots,P-1\) are the scheduled ones. From super-step
\(P-1\) on, every edge is active, so by Lemma 1 the number of components
with an outgoing edge halves in every round; after
\(\lceil \log_2 n \rceil \le tP\) further rounds, i.e. at most \(P\)
further super-steps, none remains and curEdges is empty.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Selection cost]
The aggregate cost of all nth\_element calls is
\(O(mP) = O(m\log^{1/3} n)\) in expectation, and in the worst case if
selection is done by median-of-medians or by the radix-based partition.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Super-step \(i\) partitions the unactivated suffix of size
\(m - i\cdot k\) in expected linear time. Summing over steps
\(0,\dots,P-2\) (the final scheduled step activates all remaining edges
without a partition call):
\(\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}(m - i\cdot k) \le mP = O(m\log^{1/3} n)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Phase A cost]
The aggregate cost of all Phase A activation loops is \(O(m)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each original edge is activated exactly once (curActivated advances
monotonically), and each lookup through origToComp and the DSU costs
\(O(1)\) under the cost model of Section~3.6.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Phase B cost]
The total cost of all Phase B rounds is \(O\!\left(t \sum_i M_i\right)\),
where \(M_i \le m_{i-1}' + k_i\) and \(k_i\) is the number of edges
newly activated in super-step \(i\) (\(\sum_i k_i = m\),
\(m_{-1}' = 0\)).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each round scans curEdges once and performs \(O(1)\) DSU operations per
edge. curEdges never grows within Phase B and there are at most \(t\)
rounds, so super-step \(i\) costs \(O(t \cdot M_i)\). At the start of
super-step \(i\), curEdges holds the \(m_{i-1}'\) carried edges and the
\(k_i\) newly activated ones.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Phase C cost]
The total cost of all compress() calls is
\(O\!\left(\sum_i M_i + P\cdot n\right)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each call performs an \(O(M_i)\) scan to drop internal edges, \(O(C_i)\)
work to enumerate roots and build the renaming map, an \(O(m_i')\)
renaming of the carried edges, an \(O(n)\) origToComp update and an
\(O(C_i)\) reset. Summing over the \(O(P)\) super-steps of Lemma 2 gives
the bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Worst-case bound]
BMSBoruvka computes an MST (or MSF) in \(O(m\log n)\) time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Unconditionally \(m_{i-1}' \le m\), so \(M_i \le m + k_i\) and
\(\sum_i M_i = O(Pm)\) over the \(O(P)\) super-steps. By Lemma 5,
Phase B costs \(O(tPm) = O(m\log n)\); the other phases cost
\(O(mP + nP)\), which is no larger.
\end{proof}

We now state the condition under which the improved bound holds.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Condition (H) (bounded carry-over).} There is a
constant \(c\) such that, for every super-step \(i\), the number of
carried edges is at most \(c\) times the number of components they
touch: \(m_i' \le c \cdot A_i^{(t)}\).
\medskip

Condition (H) says the carried graph has bounded average degree. It
does not hold for every graph: after compression, a sparse graph can
become dense among the few components that remain. It holds trivially
when no edges are carried at all, which is what we observed on every
input in Section~5.

\begin{theorem}[Bound under Condition (H)]
If Condition (H) holds, BMSBoruvka computes an MST (or MSF) in
\(O(m\log^{2/3} n + n\log^{1/3} n)\) time, i.e.
\(O(m\log^{2/3} n)\) for connected graphs.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Condition (H) and Lemma 1, \(m_i' \le c\,A_i^{(t)} \le c\,n/2^{t}\).
Over the \(O(P)\) super-steps of Lemma 2,
\[
\sum_i M_i \;\le\; \sum_i k_i + \sum_i m_{i-1}'
\;\le\; m + O\!\left(P\cdot \frac{c\,n}{2^{t}}\right)
\;=\; m + o(n),
\]
since \(P/2^{t} \to 0\). Collecting the costs in Table 2 gives the
bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where the improvement comes from]
Under Condition (H), Phase B scans each edge \(O(t)\) times instead of
the \(O(\log n)\) times of plain Borůvka, because an edge is scanned
only in the super-step that activates it and, with bounded carry-over,
in few later ones. The improvement over Borůvka's \(O(m\log n)\) is
therefore a factor of \(\log^{1/3} n\) under Condition (H), and none in
the worst case. The per-super-step shrinkage \(\xi_i = C_i/C_{i+1}\)
reported in Section~5 need not reach \(2^{t}\) and is not used by this
analysis.
\end{remark}

\section{Benchmark}

\subsection{Set up}

All programs were compiled with \texttt{g++ -O2 -std=c++17}, and the
parallel variant additionally with \texttt{-fopenmp}. The following
algorithms were evaluated:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{bms.} BMSBoruvka with nth\_element selection, the algorithm
  analysed in Section~4.
\item
  \textbf{boruvka.} Standard sequential Borůvka, implemented with the
  same DSU and edge-comparison routines as BMSBoruvka. Serves as the
  primary baseline.
\item
  \textbf{par\_Tk.} OpenMP-parallelised Borůvka with \(k\) threads
  (\(k \in \{2, 4\}\)). Phase 1 parallelises the cheapest-edge scan with
  thread-local best-edge arrays; Phase 2 reduces them sequentially;
  Phase 3 unions sequentially.
\item
  \textbf{kruskal.} Kruskal's algorithm: a full sort of the edges by
  weight followed by a DSU scan.
\item
  \textbf{filter\_kruskal.} Filter-Kruskal {[}OSS09{]}: partitions the
  edges around a pivot weight, recurses on the lighter part first, and
  discards heavier edges whose endpoints are already connected before
  sorting them.
\item
  \textbf{prim.} Prim's algorithm with a binary heap (lazy deletion),
  restarted from an unvisited vertex to produce a spanning forest on
  disconnected inputs.
\end{enumerate}

Every implementation was checked against the others on every test
graph: all algorithms report the same total MST/MSF weight.

\textbf{Test graphs.} Graphs were produced by a random generator that
draws each edge's endpoints uniformly at random (no self-loops; parallel
edges may occur) and assigns weights from one of three distributions:
\emph{random} (uniform integers in \([1, 10^{9}]\)), \emph{small}
(uniform integers in \([1, 100]\), many ties) and \emph{uniform} (all
weights equal to 1). Unless stated otherwise weights are random. Each
case was generated with seeds 42, 43 and 44, and every algorithm was
timed on all three instances. The cases fall into four groups:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\Alph{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Group A (average degree \(2\ln n\)).}
  \(n \in \{10^4,\ 3\cdot 10^4,\ 10^5,\ 3\cdot 10^5,\ 10^6\}\),
  \(m = n \ln n\). This density lies just above the connectivity
  threshold of random graphs, and is used for the scaling series.
\item
  \textbf{Group B (average degree \(\sqrt{n}/2\) and \(2\sqrt{n}\)).}
  \(n \in \{3\cdot 10^4,\ 10^5\}\), for comparison across densities.
\item
  \textbf{Group C (weight distributions).} \(n = 10^6\), \(m = n\ln n\)
  as in Group A, with \emph{uniform} and \emph{small} weights; the Group
  A graph with \(n = 10^6\) serves as the control.
\item
  \textbf{Group D (legacy, \(m = 5n\)).} \(n = 10^6\) with random,
  uniform and small weights. This density lies below the connectivity
  threshold, so these graphs embed a random Hamiltonian path to make
  them connected; they are kept only for comparison with earlier
  measurements.
\end{enumerate}

Groups A--C are not forced to be connected; at their densities the
generated graphs are connected with high probability, and all
algorithms return a spanning forest otherwise.

\textbf{Timing.} Each program measures its own MST computation with
\texttt{std::chrono::steady\_clock}, starting after the graph has been
read into memory and stopping before any output is written, so parsing
and output are excluded. Within one process the computation is repeated
\(K\) times on the same parsed graph, and the first repetition is
discarded as a warm-up; algorithms that modify their input receive a
fresh copy before each repetition, outside the timed region. \(K\) is
chosen per test case so that the total measurement time stays within a
fixed budget, with \(3 \le K \le 60\). All measurements therefore
reflect warm caches. Experiments ran on a single exclusive node of
\textbf{[TBD: cluster, CPU model, core count, memory]}.

\subsection{Metrics}

\textbf{Running time.} For each case and algorithm we report the mean
of all timed repetitions pooled over the three graph instances, the
standard deviation \(\sigma\) of individual repetitions, and
\(\sigma_{\text{inst}}\), the standard deviation of the three
per-instance means, which measures variation between random graphs.

\textbf{Shrinkage factor \(\xi\).} We define
\(\xi_i = C_i / C_{i+1}\), where \(C_i\) is the number of components
before step \(i\); a step is one round for Borůvka and one super-step
for BMSBoruvka. The final step always ends at \(C = 1\) and is
therefore truncated; including it would make the geometric mean over
\(s\) steps exactly \(n^{1/s}\), whatever the shrinkage pattern. We
therefore report \(\xi_{\text{full}}\), the geometric mean over the
non-final steps, together with the number of steps, which for
BMSBoruvka we compare with \(P = \lceil \log_2 n / t \rceil\). For
Borůvka we also report the smallest non-final \(\xi_i\), which must be
at least 2. \(\xi\) depends only on the graph and the algorithm's
schedule, not on the machine. As noted in Section~4.3, a super-step of
BMSBoruvka need not shrink the component count by \(2^{t}\), and the
analysis does not rely on it.

\textbf{Edge work.} To relate running time to the analysis, we report
the number of edge scans per input edge: \(t \cdot \sum_i M_i / m\)
for BMSBoruvka (an upper bound, since rounds can stop early) and the
number of rounds for Borůvka, which scans all \(m\) edges in every
round. We also report the largest number of edges carried from one
super-step into the next, \(\max_i m_i'\), and the largest ratio
\(m_i' / A_i^{(t)}\), which is the constant \(c\) of Condition (H).

\textbf{Normalised throughput \(\widehat{t}\).} We define
\(\widehat{t} = \theta_0(n, m) / T_{\text{mean}}\), where
\(T_{\text{mean}}\) is the mean running time in milliseconds and
\(\theta_0\) is the operation count of the algorithm's bound:

\begin{quote}
bms: \(\theta_0 = m \cdot (\log_2 n)^{2/3}\)
\quad \([O(m \log^{2/3} n)]\)

all other algorithms: \(\theta_0 = m \cdot \log_2 n\)
\quad \([O(m \log n)]\)
\end{quote}

Both carry the factor \(m\), so \(\widehat{t}\) is comparable across
densities and between algorithms. If \(\widehat{t}\) approaches a
constant as \(n\) grows, the running time scales as the bound predicts;
a falling \(\widehat{t}\) indicates costs growing faster than the bound,
for example from cache effects.

6--7\% at n = 1000000. The overhead arises from three sources: (i)
allocation and initialisation of T × n thread-local best-edge arrays per

reduction over all T × n entries; and (iii) OpenMP thread-launch
latency. With only two physical cores, the parallel workload in Phase 1
is insufficient to amortise these costs. On machines with 16 or more
cores, where Phase 1 can distribute m edge operations over many threads,
the parallel variant is expected to show positive speedup.

to a theoretical complexity advantage: on dense graphs Phase B degrades
to O(m log n) regardless of algorithm, so both boruvka and BMSBoruvka
are asymptotically equivalent. The practical speedup observed here stems
from BMSBoruvka's fewer total rounds (due to larger per-super-step
shrinkage) rather than from reduced edge-scan work per round.

\(\widehat{t}\) stabilises, confirming that the RAM model captures the
asymptotic regime correctly even though it does not model cache effects.

Dense graph behaviour. For dense graphs, \({\widehat{t}}_{bms}\) drops
to approximately 260--270, far below the sparse-graph values. This is an
artefact of the normalisation: \({\widehat{t}}_{bms}\) uses
\(nlog^{\frac{2}{3}}n\) as its theoretical operation count, which is
derived under the sparse assumption m = O(n). On dense graphs Phase B
costs O(m log n) (Theorem 2), not
\(O\left( nlog^{\frac{2}{3}}n \right)\). Normalising by the smaller
sparse formula therefore makes \({\widehat{t}}_{bms}\) appear
artificially low. This is precisely the intended behaviour:
\({\widehat{t}}_{bms} \ll {\widehat{t}}_{boruvka}\) on dense graphs
serves as an empirical signal that the theoretical advantage of
BMSBoruvka does not apply, consistent with the worst-case bound of
Theorem 2.

\subsection{Comparison with Classical MST Algorithms}

\textbf{Classical algorithms} (\textbf{Borůvka}~{[}Bor26{]},
\textbf{Kruskal}~{[}Kru56{]}, \textbf{Prim}~{[}Pri57{]}). The three
textbook algorithms all run in \(O(m \log n)\) or \(O(m + n \log n)\)
time under the comparison model. Borůvka's algorithm~{[}Bor26{]}, the
structural foundation of BMSBoruvka, iterates rounds in which every
component simultaneously selects its cheapest outgoing edge; after ⌈log₂
n⌉ rounds a single component remains. Kruskal's algorithm~{[}Kru56{]}
sorts all edges once and greedily adds them in order, with cost
dominated by the \(O(m \log m)\) sort. Prim's algorithm~{[}Pri57{]},
when implemented with a Fibonacci heap~{[}FT87{]}, achieves the best
classical bound of \(O(m + n \log n)\), which is tighter on dense
graphs but equivalent to Borůvka on sparse ones where \(m\  = \ O(n)\).
All three are straightforward to implement and have been standard in
pedagogy and practice for decades. Under Condition (H), BMSBoruvka
runs in \(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) and so improves on all three by a factor
of \(\log^{1/3} n\); in the worst case it matches their \(O(m \log n)\)
bound.

\textbf{Fredman \& Tarjan} (1987)~{[}FT87{]}. Besides the
\(O(m + n \log n)\) bound for Prim's algorithm, Fredman and Tarjan gave
an MST algorithm running in \(O(m\,\beta(m,n))\) time, where
\(\beta(m,n) = \min\{\, i : \log^{(i)} n \le m/n \,\}\). It grows Prim
trees with a bounded heap size and contracts them between passes. For
every graph in our experiments \(\beta(m,n) \le 2\), so the algorithm is
effectively linear there and asymptotically faster than BMSBoruvka. It
is rarely implemented in practice because Fibonacci heaps carry large
constant factors. BMSBoruvka does not improve on this bound; its
contribution is within Borůvka-style algorithms.

\textbf{Karger, Klein \& Tarjan} (1995)~{[}KKT95{]}. Karger, Klein, and
Tarjan gave the first linear-time MST algorithm, achieving \(O(m)\)
expected time under the comparison model by combining random edge
sampling with a linear-time MST verification subroutine. The algorithm
is optimal in expectation: no comparison-based algorithm can do better
than \(\Omega(m)\) in expectation. However, its reliance on randomness
is a meaningful drawback. The algorithm consumes \(O(m)\) random bits
and uses MST verification as a black box, making it substantially more
complex to implement correctly than the classical algorithms or
BMSBoruvka. In practice, the large constant factors and the overhead of
the verification step mean that the algorithm is rarely competitive on
realistic inputs despite its superior asymptotic bound. BMSBoruvka,
while asymptotically slower on general graphs, is deterministic,
requires no random bits, and is faster in practice on the sparse graph
inputs most common in engineering applications.

\textbf{Chazelle} (2000)~{[}Cha00{]}. Chazelle's algorithm is the best
known deterministic MST algorithm in the comparison model, running in
\(O(m\ \alpha(m,\ n))\) time, where α is the inverse Ackermann function.
Since \(\alpha(m,\ n) \leq 4\) for any input that fits in computer
memory, this is effectively \(O(m)\) in practice. The algorithm is built
around the soft heap, an approximate priority queue that deliberately
corrupts a small fraction of its keys in exchange for \(O(1)\) amortised
operation time. Despite its elegance, Chazelle's algorithm has never
been implemented in practice: the soft heap introduces enormous constant
factors, the algorithm is designed for a pointer machine rather than a
standard RAM, and the correctness argument is extremely delicate.
BMSBoruvka is asymptotically inferior to Chazelle
(\(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) under Condition (H) vs \(O(m\,\alpha(m,n))\))
but offers something Chazelle's algorithm does not: a working
implementation. Using only a DSU and std::nth\_element, BMSBoruvka
achieves its bounds with a codebase under 400 lines of standard C++,
verified correct on 3,000 random test cases.

\textbf{Pettie \& Ramachandran} (2002)~{[}PR02{]}. Pettie and
Ramachandran proved that the algorithmic complexity of MST equals its
decision-tree complexity, presenting a deterministic algorithm running
in \(O\left( T^{*}(m,n) \right)\) time, where \(T^{*}(m,n)\) is the
minimum number of edge-weight comparisons needed to determine the MST on
any graph with n vertices and m edges. This algorithm is provably
optimal in the comparison model. The current best bounds on \(T^{*}\)
are \(\Omega(m) \leq T^{*}(m,n) \leq O\left( m\alpha(m,n) \right)\), so
the exact complexity of MST remains unknown. The algorithm is purely
theoretical: it operates by precomputing optimal decision trees for all
graphs up to a fixed size r, and the constant factor is exponential in
r. No practical implementation exists or is expected. BMSBoruvka makes
no claim to theoretical optimality, but its \(O(m \log n)\)
worst-case bound and its \(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\) bound under Condition
(H) are derived rigorously, and the algorithm runs correctly on inputs
of up to
\(n = 10^{6}\) vertices in under 3 seconds on commodity hardware.

\subsection{Positioning of BMSBoruvka}

The comparison above reveals a clear division in the MST algorithm
landscape. On one side are the theoretically superior
algorithms---Fredman-Tarjan~{[}FT87{]} (\(O(m\,\beta(m,n))\)),
Karger-Klein-Tarjan~{[}KKT95{]} (\(O(m)\) expected) and
Chazelle~{[}Cha00{]} (\(O(m\,\alpha(m,n))\))---that achieve lower
asymptotic bounds but are either randomised, impractical to implement,
or both. On the other side are the classical practical
algorithms---Borůvka~{[}Bor26{]}, Kruskal~{[}Kru56{]}, and
Prim~{[}Pri57{]}---that are widely deployed but plateau at
\(O(m \log n)\).

BMSBoruvka occupies a distinct middle position. It keeps the
\(O(m \log n)\) worst-case bound of Borůvka's algorithm, and under
Condition (H) runs in \(O(m \log^{2/3} n)\), which is asymptotically
better than every classical algorithm by a factor of
\(\log^{1/3} n\). It is deterministic, requires no random bits, and its
implementation uses only standard data structures available in any
modern programming environment. Empirically, it is consistently faster
than sequential Borůvka (Section~5)
\textbf{[TBD: speedup range and comparison with Kruskal /
Filter-Kruskal from the new runs]}.

Without Condition (H), BMSBoruvka falls back to \(O(m \log n)\),
matching Borůvka~{[}Bor26{]}. The \(\log^{1/3} n\) advantage comes from
scanning each edge in few super-steps rather than in every round; when
many edges are carried from one super-step to the next, each is scanned
repeatedly and the algorithm reverts to the same asymptotic regime as
its classical predecessors. This limitation is consistent with the
behaviour of all known sub-\(O(m \log n)\) MST algorithms: they either
require randomness~({[}KKT95{]}), rely on heavy data
structures~({[}FT87{]}), or are impractical~({[}Cha00{]},~{[}PR02{]}).
